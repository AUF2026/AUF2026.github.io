 ## 11\. Falsifiability and Structural Constraints

 A structural framework should not be formulated so broadly that every possible observation can be interpreted as confirmation.

 The fundamental methodological requirement is:

 $$
\text{structure} \Rightarrow \text{constraints}
$$

 A meaningful structural model should therefore generate identifiable consequences that can, at least in principle, distinguish it from alternative structures.

 The stronger the structural claim, the more specific its independently testable consequences should be.

 This transforms the invariant from a purely descriptive quantity into a possible criterion of discrimination.

 ## 12\. The Problem of Underdetermination

 An observation may be compatible with more than one structure.

 For example:

 $$
\mathcal{F}_1 \sim_I \mathcal{O}
$$

 and:

 $$
\mathcal{F}_2 \sim_I \mathcal{O}
$$

 may both hold while:

 $$
\mathcal{F}_1 \neq \mathcal{F}_2
$$

 and:

 $$
I(\mathcal{F}_1) = I(\mathcal{F}_2)
$$

 In such a situation, the observation does not uniquely determine the underlying structure.

 Consequently, closure must always be stated relative to an explicitly defined invariant family.

 The relevant question is not only whether an invariant can be reconstructed, but also whether that invariant is sufficiently discriminating among competing structures.

---

 ## 13\. The Problem of the Chosen Invariant

 An invariant is not automatically fundamental merely because it is conserved.

 A formally correct invariant may still be structurally weak if it carries little discriminatory information.

 A structurally significant invariant should therefore be:

 - formally defined;
- demonstrably preserved by the relevant maps;
- sufficiently discriminating;
- reproducible;
- independently testable;
- not dependent solely on the procedure used to discover it.

 The identification of suitable invariants is consequently a central mathematical and methodological task of the framework.

 ## 14\. The Role of Computation

 Computation occupies an intermediate position in the architecture.

 An algorithm may:

 - reveal structural properties;
- test formal consequences;
- construct representations;
- generate realizations;
- compare alternative structures.

 However:

 $$
\text{computability} \neq \text{fundamentality}
$$

 and:

 $$
\text{computational complexity}
\neq
\text{ontological depth}
$$

 An efficient or formally verified algorithm does not, by itself, establish that the corresponding structure is physically or ontologically fundamental.

 Computation is therefore treated as a mechanism for realization, analysis, and verification within the framework.

 $$
\text{formalization}

\text{predictions}

\text{measurability}

\text{experimental testing}

 The framework therefore distinguishes:

 $$
\text{mathematical validity}
\neq

 A mathematically consistent structure may still require independent physical evidence before it can be interpreted as a fundamental description of nature.

 1. Formal consistency

3. Representational fidelity
4. Computational correctness
5. Independent robustness

 ## 17\. Research Program

 For any particular structure $\\mathcal{F}$, the framework leaves a set of concrete questions:

 1. What exactly is the invariant family $I$?
2. Which invariants can be mathematically proved?
3. Are the representations faithful with respect to $I$?
4. Do the implementations preserve $I$?
5. Are different manifestations independently realizable?
6. Are the observations sufficiently discriminating?
7. Is reconstruction independent of construction?
8. Are alternative structures compatible with the same observations?
9. Which consequences are specifically predictive?
10. Which observations or results could falsify the proposed correspondence?

 These questions define the transition from philosophical formulation to mathematical, computational, and empirical research.

 Within this framework, **foundational** does not mean "explains everything".

 It means that a structural level is proposed as a common basis from which different descriptions can be derived, compared, and related.

 The relevant property is therefore:

 $$
\text{continuity without identity}
$$

 The structure may remain invariant while:

 - representations change;
- algorithms change;
- computational implementations change;
- physical substrates change;
- observational descriptions change.

 The invariant provides the criterion for determining whether the resulting descriptions remain structurally connected.
